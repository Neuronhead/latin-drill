The six noun cases

A noun’s ending tells you its job in the sentence; word order does not. Endings below are 1st declension (puella, girl).

CaseQuestion it answersCore jobSg.Pl.Example
NominativeWho/what does it?Subject of the verb-a-ae Puella cantat. The girl sings.
GenitiveOf whom/what?Possession, “of”-ae-ārum Rosa puellae. The rose of the girl.
DativeTo/for whom?Indirect object: the receiver or beneficiary-ae-īs Puellae rosam dō. I give a rose to the girl.
AccusativeWhom/what? Where to?Direct object; destination of motion-am-ās Puellam videō. I see the girl.
AblativeBy/with/from/in/on what?Circumstances: means, company, origin, place, time-ā-īs Cum puellā ambulō. I walk with the girl.
Vocative(Direct address)Calling someone by name-a-ae Puella, venī! Girl, come!
Compressed: nominative = doer · accusative = target · dative = receiver · genitive = owner · ablative = circumstances · vocative = name called.
Trouble spots
1. The direct object goes in the accusative

The thing acted on takes -am / -ās, even when English gives no signal.

  • fortuna volō ✗ → Fortūnam volō ✓ “I want luck”
  • Test: ask “what is being wanted/given/seen?” That noun is accusative.
2. Agreement: case + number + gender, all three

Adjectives and demonstratives copy all three from their noun. Endings need not look alike across declensions.

  • fortūnam bonam: fem. acc. sg. + fem. acc. sg.
  • huic cōnsiliō: neut. dat. sg. + neut. dat. sg.
  • A demonstrative can settle an ambiguous noun: cōnsiliō alone is dative or ablative; huic is dative only.
3. In + accusative vs in + ablative

Accusative = where to. Ablative = where at or where from.

LatinCaseMeaning
ex urbeablativefrom the city
in urbeablativein the city (already there)
in urbemaccusativeinto the city (arriving)
in terrāablativeon the land
in terramaccusativeonto the land

English splits these with two prepositions (in/into, on/onto). Latin uses one preposition and changes the noun ending.

4. Prepositions take only accusative or ablative

Never nominative or vocative. A one-case preposition tells you the case before you read the ending.

CasePrepositions

Ablative onlyā/ab, cum, dē, ē/ex, prae, prō, sine

4th conjugation vs 3rd -iō verbs

Both groups keep an -i- through the present, future and imperfect, so they look alike. Agō (plain 3rd) is shown for comparison.

Form3rd: agō3rd -iō: capiō4th: audiō
Infinitiveagerecapereaudīre
Present I / youagō, agiscapiō, capisaudiō, audīs
Present we / theyagimus, aguntcapimus, capiuntaudīmus, audiunt
Future I / heagam, agetcapiam, capietaudiam, audiet
Imperfect Iagēbamcapiēbamaudiēbam
Imperative sg. / pl.age, agitecape, capiteaudī, audīte
  • Tell them apart by the infinitive (2nd principal part): -īre = 4th (audīre, venīre). -ere with -iō in the first part = 3rd -iō (capiō, capere). -ere with plain -ō = ordinary 3rd (agō, agere).
  • Short -i- vs long -ī-. 3rd -iō verbs have a short i where the 4th has a long one: capis / audīs, capimus / audīmus, capitis / audītis. Without macrons they look the same; the infinitive settles it.
  • Imperfect: two vowels before -bā-. Both groups use -iēbā-: audiēbam, capiēbam, never *audībam.
  • Future uses -ia- / -iē-, like agō: audiam, audiēs, audiet. Watch audiet (he will hear) against audit (he hears), and capiet against capit.
  • Four short imperatives drop the final -e in the singular: dīc (say), dūc (lead), fac (do, make), fer (bring). Plurals: dīcite, dūcite, facite, and ferte.
Personal pronouns: ego, tū, is

A pronoun stands in for a noun and takes whatever case that noun would have. 1st person = the speaker (ego, nōs); 2nd = the person spoken to (tū, vōs); 3rd = the person or thing spoken about (is, ea, id).

  • Subject pronouns are for emphasis. The verb ending already says who: labōrō = I work. Ego or tū appears only when the subject is stressed or contrasted: Ego labōrō, tū dormīs. I am working, you are asleep.
  • Cum goes on the end of a personal pronoun: mēcum (with me), tēcum (with you), nōbīscum (with us), vōbīscum (with you all).
  • "My, your, our" are adjectives, not the genitives of ego/tū: meus, tuus, noster, vester, agreeing with the thing owned. Frāter meus = my brother (never frāter meī).
  • "His, her, its, their" are genitives of is and never change to agree: fīlia eius = his/her daughter, fīliās eius = his/her daughters, librī eōrum = their books (men or mixed), librī eārum = their books (women).
  • Latin often leaves possessives out unless they are needed for emphasis or clarity; English usually supplies them: Mātrem amat. He loves his mother.
  • Is as "this / that". Standing alone it is a pronoun (eam videō, I see her). Directly with a noun it agrees and means this/that, weaker than hic or ille: eam puellam videō, I see that girl.
Īdem, eadem, idem (the same)
  • Build it from is + -dem: eiusdem, eīdem, eōdem, eaedem, eōsdem. Three adjustments: masc. nom. īdem (not *isdem), neut. nom./acc. idem (not *iddem), and -m becomes -n before -dem: eundem, eandem, eōrundem, eārundem.
  • Like other demonstratives it works alone or with a noun: eōsdem librōs legimus, we read the same books.
Tricky words: nēmō, autem
  • Nēmō (no one) borrows from nūllus. Singular only: nēmō, nūllīus, nēminī, nēminem, nūllō (or nūllā). Filling gaps in one word with forms of another is called suppletion, as in English go / went.
  • Autem = however or moreover. It never comes first in its clause. Choose however when the new statement cuts against what came before, moreover when it adds to it.
The perfect active system

Three more tenses — perfect, pluperfect, future perfect — all built the same way in every conjugation: perfect stem + endings. They look at an action as completed.

Principal parts and the perfect stem

A verb's four principal parts: 1 present (laudō, I praise) · 2 infinitive (laudāre, to praise) · 3 perfect (laudāvī, I praised / have praised) · 4 perfect passive participle (laudātum, praised). Parts 3 and 4 follow no single rule, so learn all four with each verb.

Principal partsPerfect stem

moneō, monēre, monuī, monitummonu-
agō, agere, ēgī, āctumēg-
capiō, capere, cēpī, captumcēp-
audiō, audīre, audīvī, audītumaudīv-
sum, esse, fuī, futūrumfu-
possum, posse, potuī, —potu-

Rule: drop the final -ī of the 3rd principal part. Some verbs have no 4th part (possum); some give a future participle instead (sum → futūrum, about to be). Practise them in Verb Conjugations → Tenses → Principal parts.

  • Future perfect = perfect stem + the future of sum (erō, eris, erit …), except the 3rd plural: -erint, not -erunt.
  • Three look-alike "they" forms: laudāvērunt (they praised, perfect) · laudāverant (they had praised) · laudāverint (they will have praised). The perfect also has a short alternative, laudāvēre, mostly in poetry.
What the tenses mean

  • Imperfect vs perfect. The imperfect shows an action as ongoing or repeated: scrībēbat, he was writing / kept writing / used to write. The perfect shows it as done: scrīpsit, he wrote (once) or he has written (and the letter now exists). Choose "has" when the result still matters now, the simple past otherwise.
Synopsis

A synopsis tests a whole verb quickly: one person and number through all six indicative tenses, in the order present, future, imperfect, perfect, future perfect, pluperfect. Example, laudō in the 1st person plural:

Other notes

Smaller points from questions so far. New topics get added here or as new sections.

  • Idioms translate as a unit. In matrimōnium dūcere is literally “to lead into marriage” and idiomatically “to marry” (a man as subject). The literal version proves the parse; check which your course expects.
  • Two verbs spelled volō. Volō, velle = want (irregular). Volō, volāre = fly (1st conj.). Context decides.
  • 3rd-conjugation future looks like a 2nd-conjugation present. Dūcet = he will lead (future: -am, -ēs, -et). The present is dūcit. Compare monet (2nd conj.), which is present.
  • Hic vs hīc. Hic = this man (pronoun). Hīc = here (adverb). Macrons settle it.
  • Commands: singular imperative Optā!, plural Optāte! (Optā mihi fortūnam, wish me luck).
